Even though the heater itself contains 40 to 80 gallons, the leak is not capped at that amount. Your supply line keeps feeding the tank, which means it fills back up just as quickly as water escapes through a rusted bottom. Nothing stops the flow except shutting the cold water valve. The location of the heater sets the damage. It may be a hallway closet, a garage floor or, the worst case, an attic over finished rooms.

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the Heat SourceElectric units get shut down at the breaker. With a gas heater, the knob on the control goes to the off position. Never leave a heating element or burner running on an empty tank.
  2. Close the Cold SupplyFind the cold water shutoff above the heater and turn it clockwise. A stuck or leaking valve means going to the house main and shutting the water off there.
  3. Beware of Scalding WaterWater from the tank can scald. Keep children and pets away, and do not wade through pooled water until it has had time to cool.
  4. Drain the Tank SafelyA garden hose on the drain valve, if you can get to it safely, lets you send what is left in the tank outside or into a floor drain instead of across the floor.
  5. Move Stored ItemsAnything absorbent, including boxes and rugs, should come up from the wet area. Because cardboard and fabric take in water quickly, they hold dampness against the walls and the slab.

Will Insurance Pay?

Insurance and Water Heater Leak Cleanup

Water damage to belongings, walls and floors from a tank that fails suddenly is often covered as a sudden and accidental loss. Usually the unit is left out of the claim, as age related wear is viewed as a maintenance matter. If an adjuster finds signs of a long-term leak, such as rust trails or old staining, the claim can be reduced or denied because gradual leaks usually are not covered. Read your policy, take photos of the damage and the heater, and do not dispose of the old unit before the claim is settled.

Why It Happens

Why This Happens

  • Anode rods that are used up, leaving the steel inside the tank open to rust.
  • A blocked or rusted drain pan under an attic or closet heater that overflows instead of draining.
  • Years of sediment buildup and corrosion wearing a hole in the base of an old steel tank.
  • Rust at the connections above the tank, either the flex lines or the inlet and outlet fittings.
  • A drain valve that loosens, cracks or no longer closes all the way.

What Happens if You Wait

Items Stored Near the Heater

Whatever is kept by a closet or garage heater, from keepsakes to furniture and boxes, is the first thing to get wet. Cardboard falls apart, and wet contents can mold if they are not dried or discarded quickly.

Shared Garage Wall

Water crossing a garage slab soaks the bottom plate and drywall of the wall shared with the house, then moves into the flooring on the living side.

Closet Walls

Drywall surrounding a heater closet inside the house wicks water up and lets it through to the neighboring rooms. Baseboards swell and the lower wall stays wet inside.

Adjacent Room Flooring

Water leaving a heater closet runs under the door and into carpet, laminate or hardwood in the hall. Because the water is hot, wood floors swell faster and the backing on carpet can let go.
